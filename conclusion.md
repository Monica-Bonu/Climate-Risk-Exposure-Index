# Conclusion

## Climate Risk Exposure Index

---

This project built a transparent, reproducible composite index of physical climate hazard exposure for 21 countries, using observed historical data from the World Bank CCKP (ERA5, 2000–2023) and a four-step construction methodology aligned with the OECD/JRC Handbook on Composite Indicators. Every decision — indicator selection, normalisation, weighting, and aggregation — was documented with a rationale, consistent with the evidentiary standard CSRD (ESRS E1) and TCFD physical-risk disclosure requires.

The index produces a clear and geographically coherent result. India, Nigeria, and Australia rank as the three most physically exposed countries in the sample, each for distinct reasons: India through broad-spectrum exposure to heat, extreme precipitation, and drought simultaneously; Nigeria through extreme heat and drought; and Australia through maximum values on both temperature and drought indicators. A meaningful regional divide separates tropical and semi-arid countries from the European group, with the Mediterranean countries (Portugal, Greece, Spain, Italy) occupying a distinct middle tier driven by heat and seasonal water deficit. Northern and western European countries cluster at the low end of the ranking, a result consistent with their temperate climates. The sensitivity analysis confirms that the top-tier ranking is robust to weighting assumptions: India and Nigeria hold ranks 1 and 2 under all five weighting schemes tested, and the three-tier structure of the ranking is stable throughout.

Two counterintuitive results — the Netherlands ranking 16th despite its global reputation for flood risk, and the United States ranking 12th despite its climatic diversity — are explained by specific methodological boundaries rather than data errors. Both point to the same lesson: a country-level exposure index built on three headline indicators is a screening tool, not a comprehensive risk assessment. It identifies which geographies warrant closer attention; it does not substitute for the asset-level, scenario-based analysis that a full CSRD or TCFD disclosure requires.

The project also demonstrates that the transparency CSRD and TCFD demand is practically achievable. Producing an auditable index from raw publicly licensed data — with a documented correlation check, an explicit weighting rationale, and a sensitivity analysis — requires no proprietary data or opaque vendor models. The methodology is reproducible by anyone with the same data source and the notebooks provided. That reproducibility is not merely a technical property; it is the feature that makes the output credible for disclosure purposes.

The most material limitations identified for future work are three. First, the absence of a sea-level or river-discharge indicator causes a systematic underestimation of flood risk for low-lying European countries; adding this indicator is the highest-priority extension for a version 2. Second, the historical basis of the index does not satisfy the forward-looking requirement of TCFD; a scenario-based extension using CMIP6 projection data under SSP emissions pathways would complete the physical-risk picture. Third, the country-level aggregation masks sub-national heterogeneity in large, diverse economies; a site-level or sub-national application would be required for meaningful corporate asset screening.

Within its defined scope — observed, country-level, cross-sectional physical hazard exposure — the index achieves what it set out to do. It is a working, validated, open-methodology demonstration of physical climate risk screening that a sustainability analyst, ESG team, or risk function could adapt as the starting point for their own CSRD or TCFD physical-risk programme.
